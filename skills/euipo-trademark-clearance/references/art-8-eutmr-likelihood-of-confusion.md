# Art. 8.1.b EUTMR — Likelihood of Confusion Framework

## Statutory basis

**Article 8(1)(b) EUTMR (EU 2017/1001):**
> "Upon opposition by the proprietor of an earlier trade mark, the trade mark applied for shall not be registered: [...] if because of its identity with, or similarity to, the earlier trade mark and the identity or similarity of the goods or services covered by the trade marks there exists a likelihood of confusion on the part of the public in the territory in which the earlier trade mark is protected; the likelihood of confusion includes the likelihood of association with the earlier trade mark."

## The global assessment test

Likelihood of confusion must be assessed globally, taking into account all factors relevant to the circumstances of the case (SABEL v Puma, C-251/95, para. 22). The factors are interdependent: a lower degree of similarity between the marks may be offset by a higher degree of similarity between the goods/services and vice versa (Canon, C-39/97, para. 17).

## Key factors

### 1. Similarity of signs

The global assessment of the likelihood of confusion must, as regards the visual, phonetic or conceptual similarity of the marks at issue, be based on the overall impression given by the marks, bearing in mind, in particular, their distinctive and dominant components (SABEL, para. 23).

**Three dimensions:**
- **Visual** — overall impression including length, structure, dominant elements
- **Phonetic** — pronunciation, rhythm, stressed syllables
- **Conceptual** — meaning conveyed by each mark

**Average consumer** — has reasonably well-informed and reasonably observant and circumspect character; rarely has the chance to make a direct comparison but must rely on the imperfect picture of them that he has kept in his mind (Lloyd Schuhfabrik, C-342/97, para. 26).

### 2. Similarity of goods/services

Factors for assessing similarity of goods/services (Canon, para. 23):
- Their nature
- Their intended purpose
- Their method of use
- Whether they are in competition with each other or are complementary
- Their usual origin
- Their distribution channels

The fact that the goods/services fall in different Nice classes is not determinative — what matters is their actual commercial nature and relationship.

### 3. Distinctive character of the earlier mark

Marks with a highly distinctive character, either per se or because of the reputation they possess on the market, enjoy broader protection than marks with a less distinctive character (Canon, para. 18).

**Inherent distinctiveness:** marks that are invented, arbitrary, or fanciful have higher inherent distinctiveness. Descriptive or laudatory marks have weak distinctiveness.

**Enhanced distinctiveness through use:** a mark that has become widely known in the relevant sector through intensive use acquires enhanced protection even if inherently weak (Lloyd Schuhfabrik, para. 23).

### 4. Relevant public

The relevant public consists of average consumers of the goods/services at issue, who are reasonably well-informed and reasonably observant and circumspect (Lloyd Schuhfabrik, para. 26). The level of attention paid by the average consumer varies according to the category of goods/services in question — higher for specialist or expensive goods, lower for everyday consumer goods.
