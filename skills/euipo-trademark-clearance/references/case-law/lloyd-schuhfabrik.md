# Lloyd Schuhfabrik Meyer — C-342/97 (22 June 1999)

**Court:** Court of Justice (preliminary ruling)
**Topic:** The average consumer · imperfect recollection · aural similarity · assessing enhanced distinctiveness

## Facts

Lloyd Schuhfabrik ("Lloyd") and Loint's, both for shoes. The referring court asked about the relevance of aural similarity and how to assess the distinctive character of the earlier mark.

## Holding / principle

1. **The average consumer.** The average consumer of the category of goods/services is deemed to be **reasonably well-informed and reasonably observant and circumspect** (para. 26).
2. **Imperfect recollection.** The average consumer **rarely has the chance to make a direct comparison** between marks and must rely on the **imperfect picture** of them kept in mind (para. 26).
3. **Variable attention.** The level of attention is likely to **vary according to the category** of goods/services (para. 26) — higher for expensive or specialist purchases, lower for everyday items.
4. **Aural similarity can suffice.** **Mere aural similarity** between marks may create a likelihood of confusion (para. 28) — so a phonetic match matters even where the marks look different on paper.
5. **Assessing enhanced distinctiveness.** In assessing the distinctive character of a mark, account may be taken of, in particular: the **market share** held by the mark; how **intensive, geographically widespread and long-standing** its use has been; the **investment** in promoting it; and the **proportion of the relevant public** that identifies the goods as originating from a particular undertaking (para. 22–23).

## Application in a clearance analysis

- Drives **Step 2 (sign similarity)**: always run a phonetic comparison, and apply the imperfect-recollection test — consumers compare a remembered impression, not the two marks side by side.
- Calibrate the **relevant public's attention** to the goods in Step 5; a high-attention public (e.g. professional or expensive goods) reduces the risk.
- Provides the **checklist for "enhanced distinctiveness"** in Step 4 — and, crucially, flags it as something the register usually cannot prove, so mark it as an open factor for the attorney to investigate.

## Key paragraphs
- Para. 22–23 — factors for assessing distinctive character / enhanced distinctiveness
- Para. 26 — average consumer; imperfect recollection; variable attention
- Para. 28 — aural similarity alone may create a likelihood of confusion
