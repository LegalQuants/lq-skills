# Marca Mode v Adidas — C-425/98 (22 June 2000)

**Court:** Court of Justice (preliminary ruling)
**Topic:** Reputation does not create a presumption of confusion · association is not a shortcut

## Facts

Adidas, proprietor of the well-known three-stripe mark, sought to stop Marca Mode using a two-stripe motif. The referring court asked whether the **reputation** of the earlier mark, combined with a likelihood of **association**, could *presume* a likelihood of confusion.

## Holding / principle

1. **No presumption from reputation.** The reputation of a mark does **not** give grounds for **presuming** a likelihood of confusion simply because there is a likelihood of association in the strict sense (operative ruling).
2. Even for a highly reputed earlier mark, the proprietor must still **prove a likelihood of confusion**; association that merely calls the earlier mark to mind is not, by itself, a substitute for that proof.
3. Reputation/distinctiveness is a **factor that raises** the likelihood of confusion (per Canon and SABEL) — but it does not **dispense** with the requirement to establish confusion.

## Application in a clearance analysis

- Acts as a **brake on over-calling conflicts** against famous marks: a strong reputation widens protection but does not make every echo of the mark a conflict under Art. 8(1)(b).
- Keep the Art. 8(1)(b) analysis (confusion) distinct from the Art. 8(5) analysis (reputation/dilution/unfair advantage) — reputation does heavy lifting under 8(5), but under 8(1)(b) confusion must still be shown.
- Where a conflict rests mainly on "this will remind people of the famous mark", flag it as an **association** point that needs the full confusion analysis, and consider whether Art. 8(5) is the better-fitting ground.

## Key paragraphs
- Operative ruling — no presumption of confusion from reputation + association alone
