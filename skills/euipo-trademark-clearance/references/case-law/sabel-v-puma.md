# SABEL v Puma — C-251/95 (11 November 1997)

**Court:** Court of Justice (preliminary ruling)
**Topic:** The global-assessment method · distinctive and dominant components · "mere association" is not enough

## Facts

SABEL applied to register a figurative mark showing a bounding feline (a cheetah) together with the word "SABEL". Puma opposed on the basis of earlier figurative marks consisting of a bounding feline (a puma). The marks shared the same *idea* — a leaping cat — but differed in their concrete depiction and in the presence of the word element.

## Holding / principle

1. **Global appreciation.** Likelihood of confusion must be appreciated **globally**, taking into account all factors relevant to the circumstances of the case (para. 22).
2. **Overall impression, dominant components.** The global appreciation of visual, aural or conceptual similarity must be based on the **overall impression** given by the marks, bearing in mind in particular their **distinctive and dominant components** (para. 23).
3. **Distinctiveness amplifies.** The more distinctive the earlier mark, the greater the likelihood of confusion; marks with a highly distinctive character, per se or through reputation, enjoy broader protection (para. 24).
4. **Mere association is insufficient.** Where the earlier mark is **not especially well known** and consists of an image with little imaginative content, the **mere fact that the two marks are conceptually similar** (both depict a bounding feline) is **not sufficient** to give rise to a likelihood of confusion (operative ruling).

## Application in a clearance analysis

- Sets the **method** for the whole analysis: never compare elements in isolation — assess the overall impression, then weight the distinctive/dominant parts.
- A purely **conceptual** overlap (same idea) does **not** by itself create a conflict, especially where the earlier mark is weak. Use this to *downgrade* a conflict that rests only on a shared concept.
- Conversely, flag that conceptual similarity can matter more where the earlier mark is highly distinctive or well known (read with Canon and Lloyd).

## Key paragraphs
- Para. 22 — global appreciation of all relevant factors
- Para. 23 — overall impression; distinctive and dominant components
- Para. 24 — distinctiveness widens protection
- Operative ruling — mere conceptual association insufficient for a weak earlier mark
