# CP5 — Likelihood of confusion: impact of non-distinctive / weak components

CP5 is the agreed common practice on how to handle likelihood of confusion (LoC) when the marks **coincide in a component that is weak or non-distinctive**. It is the practical complement to the case-law principles (SABEL/Canon on overall impression and distinctiveness) and the EUIPO Guidelines: it tells you **how much weight a shared weak element should carry**.

## Scope

Applies where the earlier and contested marks share a coinciding element, and that element is **non-distinctive** (e.g. purely descriptive, generic, or otherwise devoid of distinctive character) or of **low distinctiveness** (weak — e.g. allusive, laudatory, or commonly used in the trade for the goods).

## How distinctiveness of the component is assessed

- The distinctiveness of the **coinciding component** is assessed against the relevant goods/services, using the same criteria as for a mark as a whole (descriptive / allusive / laudatory / arbitrary).
- The assessment is made from the perspective of the **relevant public** and may differ by **territory/language** within the EU — a term descriptive in one language may be distinctive in another.
- Distinctiveness of the marks **as wholes** is also assessed, not only the shared element.

## Agreed common-practice conclusions

- **Coincidence only in a non-distinctive component → generally no LoC.** When the marks coincide solely in an element with **no** distinctive character, that coincidence does **not**, on its own, support a finding of LoC. The differing, distinctive components carry the weight; attention shifts to them.
- **Coincidence in a weak (low-distinctiveness) component → limited weight, but not zero.** A shared weak element has a **reduced** impact on the LoC assessment. LoC will **not usually** be found on the basis of that coincidence alone — but it is still a matter of **overall assessment**: LoC can arise where, in addition, the differing elements are themselves similar, or the overall structure/length/order makes the signs highly similar, or other factors (identical goods, etc.) tip the balance.
- **The differences in the distinctive elements become decisive.** Where the only similarity is in weak/non-distinctive matter, even modest differences in the distinctive elements can be enough to exclude LoC.
- **Weakness narrows but does not abolish protection.** A weak coinciding element still counts in the overall impression; it is not simply disregarded.

## Application in a clearance analysis

- Use directly in **Step 2 (sign similarity)** and **Step 5 (global assessment)**: when a conflict rests on a **shared weak or descriptive word/element** (e.g. both marks contain a laudatory or sector-generic term), **down-weight** that coincidence and focus the analysis on the distinctive elements that differ.
- Flag explicitly when the *only* point of contact between the proposed mark and an earlier mark is a non-distinctive element — that is a strong signal to rate the conflict **lower**, consistent with CP5.
- Combine with the distinctiveness analysis (Step 4) and the case-law corpus: CP5 operationalises, for examination practice, the SABEL/Canon point that distinctiveness shapes the scope of protection.
- Caution: distinctiveness is **territory- and goods-specific** — a component weak for one set of goods or in one language may not be weak elsewhere. Keep the assessment tied to the relevant public.
