# EUIPO opposition practice — likelihood of confusion (Guidelines, Part C, Section 2)

The CJEU case-law corpus in `../case-law/` gives the *principles*. This file summarises how the **EUIPO applies them in examination** — the practice an attorney needs to predict the Office's approach. The Guidelines are organised, broadly, into the chapters below.

## 1 — General principles and methodology

- Likelihood of confusion (LoC) is assessed **globally**, weighing all relevant factors interdependently: similarity of signs, similarity of goods/services, distinctiveness of the earlier mark, distinctive and dominant elements, and the relevant public's attention.
- The comparison is made on the marks **as registered / as applied for**, not on actual use — **except** where enhanced distinctiveness or reputation is claimed *and proven*.
- **Unitary character (key EUTM-specific point):** a relative ground existing in **part of the EU** is enough to refuse the EUTM application as a whole. A finding of LoC for the relevant public in **one Member State / one language area** suffices.
- The comparison is a two-stage gate: if signs are **not similar** *or* goods/services are **not similar**, there is no LoC and the analysis stops (both a minimum of sign similarity and of G/S similarity are necessary conditions).

## 2 — Comparison of goods and services

- Applies the **Canon** factors and the Office's own practice: nature, intended purpose, method of use, complementarity, whether in competition, **distribution channels**, **relevant public**, and **usual origin**.
- **Identity** includes the case where the contested term falls entirely within a broader category of the earlier mark (or vice versa).
- **Complementarity** has a specific meaning: one is indispensable or important for the use of the other, such that the public may think the same undertaking is responsible — not merely "used together".
- Class numbers are **not** decisive; goods in different classes can be similar and goods in the same class dissimilar.

## 3 — Comparison of signs

- Visual, aural and conceptual comparison on the **overall impression**; figurative vs word vs composite handled on their own terms.
- The **beginning** of a word sign tends to attract more of the consumer's attention than the end.
- **Short signs** — differences in one or two letters weigh more heavily.
- Coincidence in a **weak or descriptive element** carries limited weight; coincidence in a distinctive element weighs more.
- **Conceptual** comparison turns on the meaning grasped by the relevant public; a clear meaning in one sign can offset visual/aural closeness (see Picasso, Mülhens in the case-law corpus).

## 4 — Distinctiveness of the earlier mark

- **Inherent** distinctiveness depends on whether the mark is descriptive, allusive, or arbitrary/fanciful for the goods.
- **Enhanced** distinctiveness through use must be **claimed and proven** by the opponent (factors: market share, intensity/duration/geographical extent of use, investment, recognition — per Lloyd).
- A mark of **low** inherent distinctiveness still enjoys a **minimum scope** of protection; weakness narrows, but does not eliminate, protection.

## 5 — Distinctive and dominant elements

- Identify any **dominant** (visually outstanding) element in each sign.
- The dominant-element shortcut applies only where the other components are **negligible** (Shaker/Limoncello); otherwise the overall impression governs.
- An earlier mark absorbed into a longer sign may keep an **independent distinctive role** (Medion), but not automatically (Bimbo).

## 6 — Relevant public and degree of attention

- The **average consumer** is reasonably well-informed and reasonably observant and circumspect.
- Attention **varies** by goods/services: higher for expensive, technical, or professional purchases; lower for everyday low-cost goods.
- Where goods target **several publics**, the public with the **lowest** level of attention is generally decisive.

## 7 — Other factors

- **Coexistence** of the marks on the market may be argued by the applicant, but is accepted only under **strict conditions** and with solid evidence (genuine coexistence, relevant territory, absence of confusion).
- Prior **EUIPO or national decisions** are not binding, though consistency is considered.
- **Family / series of marks** can raise LoC, but only on proof of use of enough marks to constitute a family (Il Ponte).

## 8 — Global assessment

- All factors are weighed together with **interdependence**: lesser sign similarity can be offset by greater G/S similarity (and vice versa); enhanced distinctiveness of the earlier mark widens protection.
- The **imperfect recollection** principle applies: the consumer rarely compares side by side.
- The conclusion is a reasoned finding on whether the relevant public, in at least part of the EU, might believe the goods/services come from the same or economically-linked undertakings.
